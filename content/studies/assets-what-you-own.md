So far in this topic we've looked at money flowing in and out each month. Now we look at what you've built up: what you own.

## What counts as an asset

An asset is something you own that has real money value. The main kinds:

- **Cash and savings:** money in the bank, mobile money, a savings plan, money in an ajo you'll receive.
- **Investments:** fixed deposits, treasury bills, shares, mutual funds, your pension balance.
- **Property:** land or a house you own.
- **Business assets:** stock, tools and equipment you use to earn.
- **Valuables:** a car, gold, electronics, but only at what you could actually sell them for today.
- **Money owed to you:** loans you've given that you genuinely expect to get back.

When you list your assets, use what they're worth **now**, not what you paid. A phone bought for ₦400,000 two years ago might sell for ₦150,000 today.

## Assets that pay and assets that cost

Not all assets help you in the same way:

- **Assets that pay you** put money in your pocket: savings earning interest, a shop that makes profit, a property you rent out, shares that pay dividends, tools that let you earn.
- **Assets that cost you** take money out every month: a car you don't earn with (fuel, repairs, insurance), a big generator, an expensive phone.

!> Wealth grows from assets that pay you. Assets that cost you are often comforts, not investments.

That doesn't mean never buy a car. It means being honest about which is which. A car you use for ride-hailing is an asset that pays. The same car used to get to the office is a comfort that costs.

## An example

Peter lists his assets:

- savings: ₦350,000,
- pension balance: ₦600,000,
- a car he could sell for ₦2,500,000,
- a laptop worth ₦200,000.

Total: **₦3,650,000**. But only ₦950,000 of it, the savings and pension, is working for him. Most of his wealth is tied up in a car that costs him money every month.
