Some bills don't care how your month went. The rent is due. The school fees are due. The loan repayment comes out on the same date. These are your fixed expenses.

## What counts as fixed

Fixed expenses are costs you're committed to, usually for the same amount each time:

- rent or housing,
- school fees (divide yearly or termly fees into a monthly figure),
- loan repayments,
- insurance premiums,
- subscriptions: TV, streaming, gym, apps,
- regular contributions you've committed to, such as an ajo or cooperative.

## Why they matter so much

Fixed costs are hard to change quickly. You can eat out less next week; you can't move house next week.

That makes them the biggest risk in your budget. If your income falls, because of a lost job, a slow business month or an illness, your fixed costs keep coming. The higher they are, the faster a bad month turns into debt.

!> The lower your fixed costs, the safer you are when income falls.

## A simple test

Add up all your fixed expenses for a month and divide by your take-home income.

Esther takes home ₦250,000. Her fixed costs are rent of ₦50,000 a month, school fees of ₦30,000 a month on average, a loan repayment of ₦25,000 and subscriptions of ₦7,000: **₦112,000** in total.

₦112,000 ÷ ₦250,000 = **45%** of her income is committed before she buys a single meal.

There's no perfect number, but the higher this percentage, the less room you have to save, and the less room you have when something goes wrong. Many people find that keeping fixed costs well under half of take-home pay gives them breathing space.

## Ways to bring them down

- **Housing:** usually the biggest. A cheaper place or sharing for a while can free up more than any other change.
- **Loans:** paying off one debt removes a fixed cost for good.
- **Subscriptions:** cancel what you don't use.
- **Before you commit:** think twice before taking on new fixed costs, like a bigger car on a loan. They're easy to add and hard to remove.
