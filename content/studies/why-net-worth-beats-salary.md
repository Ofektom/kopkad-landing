Meet Peter and Joy.

**Peter** earns **₦1,000,000** a month. He drives a new car, lives in a nice apartment and is generous at every event. Everyone assumes he's doing very well.

**Joy** earns **₦250,000** a month. She takes the bus, rents a modest flat and says "not this month" more often than her friends would like.

Now look at their net worth:

- **Peter** owns ₦1,500,000 in savings and pension. He owes ₦3,000,000: a car loan, two loan apps and money borrowed for his rent. His net worth is **−₦1,500,000**.
- **Joy** owns ₦900,000 in savings and pension and owes ₦100,000 to her sister. Her net worth is **₦800,000**.

Joy is ₦2.3 million ahead of Peter, on a quarter of his salary.

!> Salary is what you earn. Net worth is what you've built. Only one of them is wealth.

## Why salary misleads

- **A salary can disappear.** If Peter loses his job, his debts stay. His lifestyle was built on income, not on wealth.
- **A salary says nothing about spending.** A big income with bigger spending builds debt, not wealth.
- **A salary doesn't grow by itself.** Net worth can: savings earn interest, investments grow, debts shrink.

## Why net worth tells the truth

Net worth captures everything at once: how much you've saved, what you've built, and what you owe. If it's rising, your financial life is improving, whatever your income is doing. If it's falling, something needs to change, even if you just got a raise.

## Watch the trend, not the number

One net worth figure on its own isn't that useful. What matters is the direction over time.

Joy checks hers every three months:

- March: ₦650,000
- June: ₦700,000
- September: ₦760,000
- December: ₦800,000

Steady, unspectacular, and heading the right way. That's what building wealth usually looks like.
