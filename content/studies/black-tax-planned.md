"Black tax" is the name many people use for the money they send to extended family: parents, siblings, uncles, cousins. It comes from love and duty. It's also one of the biggest reasons people with good incomes never build savings.

This study isn't about giving less. It's about giving in a way you can keep up.

## Peter's story

Peter takes home ₦350,000 a month. He used to send money whenever someone asked: ₦90,000 one month, nothing the next, ₦120,000 when a cousin's school fees came up. Each time, the money came out of whatever he had, which was usually his savings. After three years of a good salary, he had nothing put aside.

Now he plans it:

- **₦35,000 a month (10%)** is his family line. His parents get ₦25,000 on the 28th, every month, without having to ask.
- **₦10,000** is kept for requests. If it isn't used, it rolls into next month's request pot.
- **Bigger needs**, like a sibling's school fees, are planned months ahead as a goal, not paid in a panic.

His family gets more over a year than before, and it's reliable. And his savings are no longer the emergency fund for everyone.

## Five habits that help

1. **Set a fixed amount** and a fixed day. Regular, predictable support is easier for everyone than surprise rescues.
2. **Say yes with a plan.** "I can send ₦15,000 this month and ₦15,000 next month" is still yes.
3. **Pay the bill, not the person,** where you can: school fees straight to the school, a hospital bill to the hospital.
4. **Help people earn.** Paying for a skill, tools or a small stock can end the need for monthly support.
5. **Never borrow to give.** A loan to help someone turns one problem into two.

!> Your savings are not the family's emergency fund. Your family line is.

## Talk about it

Many people never tell their family how their money works. A calm conversation, such as "this is what I can send every month, on this date", sets expectations and takes the pressure off both sides. People often respect a clear plan more than unpredictable generosity.
